Research Highlights

Selected contributions spanning climate overshoot, permafrost dynamics, Earth energy partitioning, and tipping point interactions — with a focus on the long-term reversibility of the Earth system under mitigation scenarios.

01

Overconfidence in climate overshoot

Global emission reduction efforts continue to fall short of Paris Agreement targets, making systematic exploration of overshoot pathways a priority for science and policy. This study shows that climate change risks after an overshoot differ substantially from a world that avoids it. Achieving declining global temperatures can limit long-term risks compared with mere stabilization, including for sea-level rise and cryosphere changes. However, strong Earth-system feedbacks could undercut temperature reversal, resulting in high near-term and continuous long-term warming. We identify a geophysical need for preventive carbon dioxide removal capacity of several hundred gigatonnes — yet technical, economic, and sustainability constraints may limit deployment at such scales.

Schleussner, C.-F., G. Ganti, Q. Lejeune, ..., N. J. Steinert, ..., et al.: Overconfidence in climate overshoot. Nature, 634, 366–373 (2024).

doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08020-9

Permafrost-thaw impacts despite the absence of a global tipping point

Arctic permafrost contains a substantial climate-sensitive carbon pool. The existence of a global tipping point — a warming threshold beyond which permafrost thaw would self-perpetuate — remains debated. This perspective argues that despite feedbacks driving rapid and irreversible thaw at local to regional scales, the accumulated Arctic permafrost response to warming remains quasilinear. Without a global tipping point, there is no safety margin within which permafrost loss is acceptable: each increment of warming exposes more land to thaw, driving detrimental local impacts and global feedbacks.

Nitzbon, J., T. Schneider von Deimling, M. Aliyeva, ..., N. J. Steinert, ..., et al.: No respite from permafrost-thaw impacts in absence of a global tipping point. Nature Climate Change (2024).

doi:10.1038/s41558-024-02011-4
03

Underestimated land heat uptake in CMIP6 models

Current global warming produces heat uptake distributed across all components of the climate system, but current-generation models deliver estimates that diverge from observations. Using fully-coupled CMIP6 experiments including an MPI-ESM version with a deep land model component, this work shows that sufficiently deep land models exhibit increased subsurface heat uptake — yielding an Earth system heat partitioning closer to observational estimates. Results highlight the importance of the land heat sink in global energy accounting.

Steinert, N. J., F. J. Cuesta-Valero, F. García-Pereira, P. de Vrese, ..., J. F. González-Rouco: Underestimated Land Heat Uptake Alters the Global Energy Distribution in CMIP6 Climate Models. Geophysical Research Letters, 51(10) (2024).

doi:10.1029/2023GL107613

Overshooting 1.5°C is fast becoming inevitable

Research post-IPCC AR6 indicates that overshooting 1.5°C is all but inevitable in the near term. Returning below this threshold requires net-negative CO2 emissions, which carries significant feasibility uncertainties. During overshoot, natural carbon sinks could be triggered in ways not yet anticipated, prolonging elevated temperatures. Widespread and potentially irreversible impacts worsen with higher magnitude and longer overshoot duration, especially for marine biodiversity, ice sheets, and tropical regions. Self-perpetuating feedbacks at sustained warming above 2°C carry considerable risk of triggering ice sheet instabilities.

Bustamante, M., ..., N. J. Steinert, ..., et al.: Ten new insights in climate science 2023/2024. Global Sustainability (2024).

05

Climate tipping point interactions and cascades

Climate tipping elements — including the Greenland Ice Sheet, AMOC, permafrost, monsoon systems, and the Amazon — may transgress critical thresholds under ongoing warming. While knowledge of individual tipping elements has improved, their interactions are less well understood. This review maps current understanding of tipping element interactions by gathering evidence from model simulations, observations, conceptual reasoning, and paleoclimate reconstructions. Key knowledge gaps are identified and future research directions outlined.

Wunderling, N., A. von der Heydt, ..., N. J. Steinert, ..., et al.: Climate tipping point interactions and cascades: A review. Earth System Dynamics, 15(1), 41–74 (2024).

doi:10.5194/esd-15-41-2024
06

Future permafrost hydrology and its far-reaching climate impacts

Inter-model differences in Arctic–subarctic climate stem partly from how permafrost hydrology is represented in land surface components. Using the MPI Earth System Model, this study shows that contrasting but equally plausible assumptions about permafrost hydrology determine whether simulated 21st-century soil moisture trends are wet or dry — with cascading effects on cloud cover, planetary energy uptake, and regional temperatures. These uncertainties affect the Earth system as far south as the tropics and highlight the need for stronger observational constraints on permafrost hydrology.

de Vrese, P., G. Georgievski, ..., N. J. Steinert, ..., V. Brovkin: Representation of soil hydrology in permafrost regions may explain large part of inter-model spread in simulated Arctic and subarctic climate. The Cryosphere, 17(5), 2095–2118 (2023).

doi:10.5194/tc-17-2095-2023
07

Earth system reversibility under temperature overshoots

Carbon dioxide removal has been proposed as a means to partially reverse climate change after a period of temperature overshoot. Using a state-of-the-art Earth system model with explicit permafrost carbon representation, this study investigates reversibility across overshoot scenarios of varying duration and magnitude. For most large-scale indicators — surface temperature, marine and terrestrial productivity, AMOC strength — the legacy of an overshoot is indiscernible from a reference case on centennial timescales. Irreversibility is found for permafrost carbon and deep ocean properties. No evidence of tipping points or self-reinforcing feedbacks was detected.

Schwinger, J., A. Asaadi, N. J. Steinert, H. Lee: Emit now, mitigate later? Earth system reversibility under overshoots of different magnitude and durations. Earth System Dynamics, 13, 1641–1665 (2022).

doi:10.5194/esd-13-1641-2022
